# SOOTBORN

## Chapter Three: Ashgate

---

**ASHGATE**
*Forty-one days after the furnace. Kael still fixes valves.*

I woke in the bunk Seren painted.

Seventeen suns on the plaster above my face. She added one every time something good happened. The last one was half finished: a circle and four rays, and then nothing, where her brush had stopped on the last ordinary morning. I had not been able to decide whether to finish it.

"You were saying her name again," said Tavi. "In your sleep."

He was sitting on the end of the bunk with his knees up and his school worksheet on them. He had slept in my bed since the fever. Neither of us had said why. Neither of us needed to.

"Was I?" I said. "Must have been a good dream."

"Liar." He did not look up. "You're a good liar, though. Top three in Cinderwell."

I took the worksheet off him. WHY THE SURFACE IS DEADLY (5 REASONS). He had done the five. Then, under them, in pencil, a sixth: *Nobody ever came back to say.* And under that, smaller: *Seren said the sky is blue. How would she know?*

I gave it back to him.

The tin was on the shelf by the door, where it had been for thirty-nine days. Authority grey, with the little stamped sun. I knew what was inside it. I checked anyway. I always checked. Ash, and the washer, and the invoice with a fresh stamp across the bottom: BALANCE OWED: 10. GRATITUDE IS BREATH.

"I didn't touch it," said Tavi behind me. "The tin. I just say good morning to it."

"That's allowed."

"Mara pinned your name to the shift board." He was watching me now. "Bank Seven."

So that was how it was going to be.

"Then I guess they want to see if I flinch," I said.

"Bay's east, through the square." Tavi pulled the blanket over his knees. "Don't let Ivo talk you into anything. And come home."

---

Row K was up before me. It always was.

On the ledge by the door of K-7 somebody had left a bottle cap painted yellow. Then a washer, painted yellow. Then a jar lid. There had been nine of them yesterday. There were eleven now. Nobody had knocked. Nobody had said anything. They just left them, the way you would leave a lamp on for somebody.

On the wall by Mara's door were the names. Thirty-one of them, in chalk, in her square forewoman's hand: everybody on Tier Six who had been committed since she made forewoman. She went over the old ones when they faded. The last one was *Holloway, S.* The chalk was still bright.

There was a week written beside every name. I knew the shift board by heart, and I ran the weeks against it without meaning to, and every one of them landed on the same thing. An audit.

They did not commit people when somebody stole. They committed people when the count came up short.

---

Ashgate Square smelled of noodle broth and lamp oil and the hot iron of the Pillar warming up for its day. The Lumen Pillar fakes the sun for us: amber at first bell, white at noon, red at last bell. It went red an hour early the day they took her. Everybody noticed. Nobody said.

At the foot of the Pillar a Tallyman was on his knees with a bucket and a wire brush.

Tallyman Sull. Grey coat, a stub of pencil behind one ear, a face like a closed door. He was scrubbing the chalk sun off the stone, the one somebody kept drawing at the Pillar's base, Seren's sun. The little rays came off one at a time.

"Move along, soot," he said, without looking up. "Nothing to see. Just cleaning up graffiti."

I took the brush out of his hand.

I did not decide to. It was in his hand, and then it was in mine, and he was looking up at me from the stones with his mouth open.

"Touch her sun again," I said, "and I will feed you this brush."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he got up, slowly, and took the pencil from behind his ear and a little book from his coat.

"Say that again, soot," said Sull. "Slower. So I get the spelling right."

He wrote it down. He took his time about it. Then he walked off across the square whistling the shift song, and I understood that I had just made a mistake I would make again.

I put the brush in his bucket. My hands were shaking. I put them in my pockets.

---

Hollis was on the bench by the east lamp, where he always was before shift.

"Kael," he said. He moved his left arm a little, as if to show me. "Took a halberd at her commitment for humming. Still can't lift it above the shoulder." He shrugged with the other one. "Worth it. We hummed her all the way to the door. She heard us. I know she did."

Old Marrow, beside him, did not look up from his tea. "They docked my family's air ration a week for humming. Gratitude is breath, the Tallyman said." He drank. "My breath, apparently. Not theirs."

Kit came past at a run with a crate on his shoulder, Freight Three's runner, the fastest mouth on the tier. He did not slow down.

"Freight Three goes up nine floors," he said as he went by. "The sign says six. You didn't hear it from me."

Bao called me over to the noodle stall and put a bowl in front of me before I could reach for my chit.

"Sit," he said. "This one's on the house. For her. She always paid in jokes. Terrible jokes." He wiped the counter that did not need wiping. "I miss them."

I ate. It burned. It was perfect. For a minute nobody said anything, which was the kindest thing anybody had done for me in weeks.

---

**BAY THREE**
*Crew Three. First bell.*

Mara was at the shift board when I came in. She looked me up and down the way she looked at a seal somebody else had seated.

"I'd have bet against it," she said. "Would've lost. Wouldn't be the first thing I've lost this month."

On the board, in her hand:

WO-114 Valve Bank 7 — rebalance — K. Brennick
WO-115 Conveyor 2 — grease — H. Morrow
WO-116 Fuse Room C — inspect — tomorrow

I initialed WO-114. Her bank. My hands.

"You held me down that day," I said.

"And I'd do it again." Mara did not look away. "She bought your breath, boy. I wasn't going to let you hand it back."

She turned to the board.

"And Kael. Whatever you see on that bank, you see it with your mouth shut."

---

Harl Morrow was elbow-deep in the gearbox of Conveyor Two. He had worked beside Seren for six years, runner and sealer, and she used to say he was the only man on the tier who listened to a pipe before he hit it.

He looked up when I came past, and stopped working.

"Kael," he said. "I'm glad you came back."

"Somebody has to fix the valves."

"She'd have—" He stopped. He looked at the grease on his hands. "Never mind. If Seven bites, bite back. For her." He put his head back into the gearbox. "And don't tell Mara I got sentimental. She'll make me grease Conveyor Two with my own hair."

---

Inspector Vale was standing by Bank Seven with his notebook open, as if he had been waiting there since the furnace.

"Brennick," he said. "Back on Bank Seven. The Authority believes in rehabilitation."

"Inspector," I said. "You look rested. Must be all that extra air."

He did not smile. I do not think his face knew how.

"Careful. Wit is a luxury item, and you're on a very tight budget." He turned a page. "Also. You threatened a Tallyman in Ashgate this morning. With a brush, I'm told. I wrote it down." He looked up. "I write everything down."

"Then you'll want to watch me work."

"I am timing you, Brennick. The board is on the wall. I trust you can read." He went back to his page. "Seren could."

---

Bank Seven had been re-stenciled since the furnace. The fat line, the forbidden one, had a name now, in fresh white paint: AUX-9.

I worked the bank the way I had worked it for ten years. Couplings, seats, the slow quarter turns. The drums came up and settled. Every gauge swung into the green.

Including AUX-9. It was not on my work order. It was not on any schematic I had ever seen. And the bank would not balance without it. It was feeding something.

Two couplings down was the clinic branch. The same branch. I could feel it under my hand like a pulse.

Vale was walking the far end of the bay with his notebook. He would be back at this bank in under half a minute.

I turned the wheel.

A quarter. Then an eighth. AUX-9 shivered, and a sliver of the Authority's air bled sideways into the clinic line and went downhill toward forty children who had breathed on their own for six weeks and would breathe a little easier for an hour.

I seated the last coupling and stepped back just as Vale came round the furnace.

"Bank Seven," I said. "Balanced, Inspector. Boring as a bread line."

He looked at the gauges for a long, long moment.

Then he wrote something down and walked away.

Somewhere across the square, through the vents, a ward fan started to spin like a storm.

---

At the end of the shift the furnace was cold. I stood in front of it.

The heat was not there, and I felt it anyway. The doors. Her hand lifted, the washer catching the fire. Eleven seconds. It did not get smaller.

I put my palm flat on the iron of the door and held it there until it hurt. I wanted it to hurt. When I took it away the shape of my hand stayed on the iron for a moment, and then it faded, like everything they burn.

Mara found me there. She had my wage chit.

"Eighteen scrip on your chit," she said. "Less two. The Authority takes the fuel out of it now. In installments."

"They're charging me for the fire that burned my wife."

"They charge for everything. That's how they keep count."

I took the chit. I said nothing.

Mara looked at me for a while.

"Good," she said. "Hate quietly. It lasts longer."

---

Dispatch was short-handed, so Mara gave me one more job: three outbound crates on the south-east dock, tags against manifests, before Freight Three took them.

Crate A was slag, two hundred kilos, heavy as sin. It matched. Crate B was spent filter cores. It smelled like the inside of a lung. It matched.

Crate C was tagged SLAG — RECLAIM DEPOT 4. It was far too light for slag. And it was cold. Cold like nothing down here is cold. The lid seals were refrigeration-grade, way above dock spec, and somebody had sanded an older tag off before stapling the new one on.

Under it there was a second slip.

HYDROPONIC GREENS, 40 KG. DELIVER: MERIDIAN TERRACE, TIER 1.

Cinderwell starts at Tier Four.

I put the slip in my pocket.

When I came back, Mara was not at the board. She was standing by Conveyor Two, which had gone quiet. Harl's rag was hanging off the rail, still wet.

"Harl isn't on tomorrow's roster," she said.

"Where did they move him?"

"Nowhere I've been told." She did not look at me. "He was her partner, Kael. Forty-one days, and now him." She turned away. "Go home. Eat something that isn't noodles. That is not a request."

---

Old Orin was sitting on an upturned bucket outside Bay Three, talking to a pipe. He talks to pipes. He says they talk back. Seren always said he was the only man on the tier who listened to the answers.

"Seven, was it?" he said, when I came past. "Fed the fat line. AUX-9, they call it now. Painted it nice and white after they burned your girl."

I stopped.

"I drew that line forty years ago," said Orin. "On paper they made me burn." He tapped the pipe with one knuckle and listened. "Your Seren asked me about it, you know. Weeks before you ever touched it. Sharp girl." He looked up at me. "Too sharp."

"What did she ask?"

"Where it went." He went back to the pipe. "There's a hatch in your bay floor. The pipe goes down before it goes up. Follow it, if you've got the stomach."

---

I went to the ward first.

The children had painted suns all over the wall, copying hers. Some were yellow. Some were blue. One was green, with a face. Under them, in Nell's neat hand: FOR SEREN, WHO BROUGHT THE AIR.

The board said BREATHING ON THEIR OWN: 40 / 40. Forty names in chalk. Tavi's was ninth. Next to the board, somebody had added a forty-first line, with no name on it. Just a little sun.

Ada was very small and very still in her cot. She was breathing on her own, slow and steady, which six weeks ago she could not do.

"Is she coming back," she said, "to fix the fan again?"

"No, sweetheart," I said. "But I will. I'm very good at fans."

Mosi gave me a drawing on the back of a ration wrapper: a woman with a sun for a head, holding hands with forty stick children. I put it inside my jacket, over my heart. High up. Where the sky would be.

---

The hatch was in the floor of Bay Three behind the cold furnace, and it was unbolted. Somebody had left it that way.

Below it ran a service corridor that had been sealed after a pressure event in the year 2231, according to the stencil. The weld bead on the seal was clean and modern, much newer than 2231, and the date had been painted over an older date that still showed underneath. Somebody had left a candle stub at the foot of the door. For whoever was on the other side.

AUX-9 ran along the wall. On it, in old chalk, faded but careful: an arrow pointing up, and two initials. O.R.

Below it, in fresh chalk, a different hand.

*STILL FLOWING. — H.M.*

Harl Morrow. Seren's partner. He had kept going after they burned her.

Past it, a collapse. Somebody had been moving the rocks; there was a neat stack to one side. Wedged between two stones was a work glove with a Crew Three tag stitched inside.

At the end of the corridor, AUX-9 met a riser as wide as a lift shaft and turned straight up into the dark. RISER 9, said the plate. FLOW TO: and then nothing. The destination had been scratched out with something sharp.

Air fell from the grille above me.

Warm air. It smelled like the cold crate: green, and alive, and like nothing in Cinderwell.

They told us the sky would strip our lungs out. They told us there was nothing up there but fire. I stood under that grille with my face turned up into air that had been somewhere green, and I understood that they had been lying to us since before I was born.

"Balance the bank," I said, out loud, to nobody. "You meant this. Didn't you, Seren."

I stood there a long time. When I left, I had decided something. I did not say it to anybody until much later, and I will say it here only once.

I was not going to break their world. I was going to climb it.

---

Ivo caught me in the square on the way home. He had his cart, and his one good boot, and the look he got when he had news and had been told not to share it.

"Last night," he said, "Lift Zero's call light came on. Green. Eleven seconds." He looked over his shoulder at the dark gate. "Nobody came down this time, Kael. Something went up."

---

Tavi was in the bunk with the lamp turned low. He did not pretend to be asleep.

"You're home," he said. "You've got the thinking face. Seren called it your scheme face."

"It's my face face."

"Ivo came by. Said the lift light was on again." He pulled the blanket up. "Said not to tell you. So obviously I'm telling you."

"Nothing's happening, Tav. Go to sleep."

"Something is." He was seven, and he looked at me the way Seren used to look at a gauge. "Then I want to be ready too. Promise you won't do it alone." He paused. "Not like last time."

I did not answer. I lay down in the bunk beside him, under the seventeen suns, and turned the lamp out.
