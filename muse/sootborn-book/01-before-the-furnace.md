# SOOTBORN

## Chapter One: Before the Furnace

---

**FOUR YEARS AFTER THE FURNACE**
*The Assay. The last day.*

Dawn, and banners.

They hang from a keep of grey stone on the end of a ridge, gold and white, and the wind has them. Below the keep the grass is wet. Above it the sky is a color I had no word for until I was nearly thirty.

A boy stands under the banners with a blade held low. He is eighteen. He has never been hit by anything he could not see coming.

On a white platform a hundred paces away, a woman sits in a chair with her hands folded and watches him.

The last day. Her son holds the keep.

I am in gold plate, with a white cloak over it that is not mine. My boots hum against my ankles. I have a blade in my hand with somebody else's steel and my own end on it, and under the plate, on a cord, I have a brass washer that has been round my neck so long it has worn a mark.

The boy lifts his blade.

The first cut comes in out of the sun, and I am not there. The second comes and I step through it. I hit, and he takes it. He hits, and I take it. Somewhere under my ribs a pressure starts, low, the way a seal starts to lift before anybody hears it.

I go up. The ground drops off me. The keep turns into a square of roof, then a square of sky, and I hang there in the wind with the whole valley under me and the woman in the chair turning her head, only her head, to follow.

Then I come down on him.

The pressure lets go.

One. Two. Three.

I know how many there will be. There are always eleven.

Eight. Nine. Ten.

The blade comes down.

---

**BEFORE THE FURNACE**
*Cinderwell. Six tiers under the rock. Before any of it.*

They say the surface burned two hundred years ago. That the sky is poison, and the sun is a furnace that eats lungs.

So we live below. Cinderwell: six tiers of rock wrapped round a machine called the Deep Array, which makes the only air there is. We keep the Array running. The Array Authority keeps the count. Every breath down here is rationed, logged and owed.

They have a word for us. Soot. The thing that is left over when something useful burns.

Once in a decade they come down Lift Zero, in white, in light. We kneel. They go back up. Nobody from Cinderwell has ever gone with them.

My name is Kael Brennick. I fixed valves on Tier Six. And eleven months before the furnace, I thought the worst thing that could happen to me was a girl saying no.

---

**ELEVEN MONTHS BEFORE THE FURNACE**
*The Festival of the First Fire. Ashgate Square.*

Once a year, Cinderwell lights paper lanterns and pretends the Lumen Pillar is the sun. It is the only night anybody stays out past last bell.

Seren was on a crate at the foot of the Pillar with a brush in one hand and a pot of gold chalk in the other, painting a sun on the stone. She had paint on her wrist and her tongue between her teeth.

"Don't look," she said. "It's not finished."

"What is it?"

"It's a sun."

"It's a lot of rays."

"It's *the* sun. The real one." She did not turn round. "Orin says it's white-gold, and you can't look straight at it, and it never needs a valve."

"I can't look straight at you either," I said. "Same problem."

The brush stopped.

"That," said Seren Holloway, "was disgusting."

"Aye."

"Say it again."

I did not say it again. I said, "Paint me one I can carry."

"Greedy." She came down off the crate and looked at me the way she looked at a gauge she did not trust. "You always want the one you can hold."

"One day I'll show you the real one."

"Don't promise me the sky, Kael Brennick." She wiped her hands on her trousers, which did not help either of them. "Promise me tomorrow. Tomorrow I can use."

I had been carrying it in my pocket for three weeks. A brass washer off Bank Seven, the size of the end of my finger, filed smooth on my lunch breaks until it shone like something that mattered. I had practised the next part on a valve wheel. The valve wheel had not been impressed either.

I went down on one knee on the hot stones, which you do not do in Ashgate Square, and held it out on my open palm.

"Seren Holloway," I said. "I can't give you the sky. But I can give you a perfectly balanced pressure seal."

The square went quiet in the way a square does when it has decided this is the most interesting thing that has happened all year.

Seren looked at the washer. Then at me. Then at the washer.

"That," she said, "is the worst proposal in the history of Cinderwell."

"Aye."

"Aye. Obviously aye." She took it off my hand and pushed it onto her own finger before I could do it for her. It went on as if it had been waiting. "Put it on me before I come to my senses. Oh. I've done it. Never mind."

Mara Venn bound us at the Pillar in front of the whole of Crew Three. She was forewoman then and she is forewoman now, and she married half the tier and buried a quarter of it, and she did not look as though she was enjoying herself until the very end. She put chalk on both our hands and pressed them together and said the words, and Seren's voice went on the last one. Nobody ever mentioned that to her.

Ivo cried louder than anybody.

"I'm not crying," he said, to anyone who would listen. "The square is dusty. The square is *very* dusty."

When it was done, Seren took my wrist and turned it over and drew the last ray of her sun on the inside of it with one finger, in chalk.

"There," she said. "Now you've seen it."

---

The next morning, Row K was up before us. It always was.

Seren stood in the door of K-7 in my coat, with the washer on her finger, and caught me looking at it.

"Don't look at it like that," she said. "It's a washer. It's the only round thing you own and you gave it away, so now you own nothing round."

"I own half of one."

"Aye. The inside half."

Mara came down the row with her sleeves rolled. "Bay Three at first bell, the both of you. I married you. I didn't give you the day."

My brother came out behind us in his nightshirt. Tavi was six. He looked at Seren, and at the ring, and at me, and frowned the way he frowned at sums.

"Is she my sister now?" he said. "Do I have to do what she says?"

"Aye," I said. "And aye."

Tavi thought about that. Then he went and stood next to her, and not next to me.

It stayed that way.

---

**FOUR DAYS BEFORE THE FURNACE**
*The fever comes to Tier Six.*

Tavi was seven, and when he breathed it sounded like a sock being pulled over a boot.

I heard it from the bunk. Our bunk, in the back room of K-7, under the wall where Seren had painted her suns. There were seventeen of them. She added one every time something good happened. Tavi counted them every night in a whisper until he fell asleep, and every night he got a different number, and every night he said that was because she had been painting more.

That night he did not count.

"Kael?" he said, from the cot. "Is it morning? It feels like breathing through a sock."

"Not morning yet, Tav. Sleep. That's an order from your favorite person."

"Seren's my favorite person."

From the floor, in the dark, Seren said, "Smart kid."

Then she said, "Kael. Get up."

---

The fever ward was on the far side of the tier, past the water. Nell ran it. She had the nurse's way of standing with her hands folded so that you could not see them shake. They were shaking.

There were forty cots. There were forty children in them, six to a row, and every one of them was breathing with its mouth open. You could hear a room full of children working for every breath, and once you had heard it you could not stop.

"I'll be honest," said Nell, "because you'd know if I wasn't. The fever isn't what kills them. It's the air."

"I know."

"The Authority cut the clinic to ration pressure a month ago. Forty kids, breathing each other's breath." She looked at the cot at the end of the row, where my brother lay with his hand flat on his chest as if he could hold the air in. "Tavi's lungs are filling."

"Then we get the clinic more air."

"From *where?* Every cubic meter in Cinderwell is counted. Tier Six doesn't get one breath more than its ration."

"Not every meter," I said. "Bank Seven has a line nobody's allowed to touch. Authority seal. Fat as my arm."

I had read its gauge once, on a slow shift when I was too bored to count bolts. It sat on the north wall of Bay Three with a gold seal on the flange and its needle in the red, and it never moved, and nobody ever asked why. That one pipe carried more air than all of Tier Six put together. Every night. Going somewhere.

"It's Authority, Kael," said Seren. "If they so much as see a needle twitch—"

"So we bleed it slow. A sliver, into the clinic line. And we balance the bank again before the morning audit. Nobody sees a thing."

Stealing air is the one crime the Authority never forgives. They do not fine you for it. They do not jail you. They commit you to the Array, and the Array runs on fire.

Nell looked at the floor for a long time.

"I didn't hear any of this," she said. "I'll have the ward vents open at second bell."

---

We walked back through the square without talking. At the Pillar Seren stopped, under her own painted sun, and turned round.

"Why you?" she said. "Why does it always have to be you?"

I could have said a great many things. That I was better at this than anybody they had. That one day I wanted to be the one who decided who breathes.

"Because I won't watch Tav drown in his own bed," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment.

"Aye," she said. "That's the answer I married, you idiot. That exact one."

Then she took me by the collar and told me how we were going to do it, the way she told me everything: twice, and then made me say it back.

"Listen. The audit relay is on the east wall, straight across the bay from the door. The grey box with the red lamp. Cut that first, or the bank logs every turn you make. Then Valve Bank Seven, on the north wall. Relay, then bank. Say it back."

"Relay, east wall. Then the bank."

"Fine. I'll keep watch. The Night Tallyman walks Bay Three every hour with a lantern. If he sees you, you're a thief. If he sees me, I'm lost."

"And if he sees both of us?"

"Then we're newlyweds looking for somewhere private." She let go of my collar. "Try to look embarrassed. Shouldn't be hard."

---

**BAY THREE**
*Second bell.*

Bay Three at night is the loudest quiet in Cinderwell. The banks breathe. The pipes knock. The heart-furnace at the far end ticks as it cools, and the whole bay smells of hot iron and old oil.

The Night Tallyman walked the middle of it, end to end, with his lantern held high. At each end he stopped and looked up and down, the way a man checks a stove he is not sure he turned off. A second lamp walked the center column and looked left and right. By the relay, the Night Watchman stood with his back to it and turned all the way round, slowly, because he had been told nobody came down this far.

The lamps swept. I counted them. Four. Six. Eight. There was always a gap, if you were patient, and nobody in Cinderwell is more patient than a valve hand.

I went along the crates on my knees. At the east wall I put my hand on the grey box and held it there while the cutter bit into the seal, two seconds, not breathing, the way I had been taught.

The lamp came round. It stopped on the crate beside my face.

Across the bay, by the door, something heavy went over with a crash, and Seren's voice said a word I will not write down.

Every lamp turned toward her.

She stood in the doorway with a sack of bolts at her feet and a hand on her hip, swearing at the sack. The Night Tallyman held his light on her for a long time. Then he grunted, and turned, and walked on down the middle of the bay, and Seren picked up her bolts and was gone.

The relay was dead.

Bank Seven came up under my hands with its drums singing. I found the Authority line by touch, the fat one, and the clinic branch two couplings down, and I turned the wheel a quarter. Then another eighth.

The forbidden line shuddered. A sliver of the Authority's air bled sideways into the clinic line and ran downhill toward forty sleeping children.

Seren was at my shoulder. I had not heard her come.

"Kael," she whispered. "Did it work?"

"Listen."

Far off, through the vents: the clinic fans, spinning up. Faster. Then faster again. And then, thin and tired and unmistakable, a cheer, from a ward full of children who could suddenly breathe.

Seren put her forehead against my shoulder.

"You're insufferable when you're right," she said into my coat.

"Then I plan to be insufferable for a very long time."

She lifted her head. She was not smiling. "Balance it back before the audit. Promise me. Every night until the fever breaks, and then never again."

"Every night," I said. "Balanced by dawn. On my breath."

She pulled me down by the collar. For a moment, in the dark of Bay Three, with the banks breathing round us and the lamps far off, Cinderwell was warm for the right reasons.
