# SOOTBORN

## Chapter Four: That Night

---

**THAT NIGHT**
*He does not dream of the furnace. For once it runs forward.*

---

**HALF A YEAR AFTER THE FURNACE**
*A sealed pump room under Tier Six. The Table.*

A woman finds me in the old tunnel.

She was Accredited once. She still has the height, and the gold in her eyes, and nothing else they gave her. Her coat is patched at the elbows. Her voice is the cleanest thing I have ever heard below Tier Four.

Her name is Ilse Marrin, and she has carried a table down a stair a piece at a time.

It stands in Pump Room Seven, under Tier Six, which has been sealed for thirty years. A table. A lamp. Straps. A spool of gold wire finer than packing thread, and I know enough about the price of things to know that spool would buy Tier Six its air for a year. She stole it. She stole it for my shins.

"They do not fear soot," says Ilse. "They fear nothing that kneels. They only fear what looks like them."

"So?"

"So I am going to make you look like them." She turns the lamp up. "It will hurt more than anything that has happened to you. Except one thing."

"Why would one of them do this for one of us?"

She does not answer straight away. She sets out instruments on a cloth in a row, very neatly, the way Mara lays out a crew's tools.

"I kept a column of the Count for eleven years," she says. "One morning I was handed a line to strike, and I asked to see the person first. That was the whole of my crime. I asked to see her." She straightens the last instrument. "She was a welder on Tier Five with a cough. They struck her while I was still on the stair. Then they struck me from my House, which is slower, and you are permitted to watch."

She looks at me.

"You may stop me at any stage until the eyes. After the eyes there is no going back down. The dark will hurt you for the rest of your time."

"I've been in the dark all my breath," I say. "Do it."

---

They call it the Gilding. They do it to their own children at twelve: gold wire run into the long bones so they grow tall, gold in the eye so they can stand the sun.

Done to a grown man, it takes nine months on a table, and most of his voice.

The straps are not for the pain. "They are for the growing," says Ilse, buckling them. "A bone that is being lengthened will try to go back to what it was." She pulls the last one tight. "So will you."

A child's bones are soft and take the wire. Mine have to be opened. I count the places. Of course I count. There are more than eleven, and I have to start again, and starting again is worse than the wire.

A finger's width a day. The wire does not stretch the bone. It gives the bone something to climb.

Then the throat. Twenty years of furnace air comes out of it in black thread. I had not known that was why everybody on my tier sounded alike. I had thought it was only how people sound.

Then she turns my hands over under the lamp.

"Now the hands," she says. "An heir has no burns on his palms. He has never held anything hot."

I close them.

"No."

"They will see them."

"Then they'll see them." My voice is not my voice. It is thin and new and it hurts. "Those are Bank Seven. Those are the night I kept forty beds breathing. Take the rest. Not those."

It is the only no I have. She looks at my hands for a long time. Then she wraps them, and I wear gloves for a year, and she never raises it again.

---

On the table I go back to one day, over and over. Not the furnace. I will not give them the furnace.

We are nine. Seren has stolen ration fuel, which is a commitment offence, and she lights it on a cold valve block behind Bank Seven with a flint and a curl of packing.

"Hands, Kael," she says. "Everything is hands. If you can make one warm thing you can make another." She grins. "Don't tell Mara."

She holds mine over it until they stop shaking. I had not known they were. She had known for a week.

Every time the wire goes in, I put my hands back over that fire. By the sixth month I can smell the packing burn.

---

Last, the eyes. Gold leaf laid behind the lens, so the sun cannot burn what it was never meant to reach. It is the only part done awake.

Then a month under wrappings, with the lamp out, growing. I am not in that room for most of it.

When the wrappings come off, Ilse is holding a bare lamp a hand from my face. It does not hurt. Nothing bright has hurt since. I have never forgiven how easy that was to give us, and how long they did not.

I go onto the table a valve tech. I come off it a head taller, with a face my own mother would walk past, and I have to learn to walk.

Ten years come off with the rest. The wire does that. It is most of what the Gilding is for.

The floor is further away than I left it. My own boots are under the cot. They will never fit again.

Ilse holds up a glass.

I look in it for her. I had thought, stupidly, that some part of it would still be the man she married, and that she would know him. There is nobody in the glass that either of us has ever met.

"You are looking for somebody," says Ilse. "Stop. He is not gone. He is underneath, where the wire is. That is the only place he was ever going to be safe."

"Was it like that for you?"

"I was twelve." She puts the glass face down on the table. "I do not remember who was underneath. That is what the Gilding is for."

---

"Now the mouth," says Ilse. "You sound like Tier Six every time you open it. Tell me you are grateful."

"Ta."

"No. Soot thanks. To thank is to admit you were given something. They say: noted." She sits down across from me. "Soot says aye. They say granted. Soot says my breath when it means my life, because you have had to count it. They have never counted a breath. They say my time."

"And?"

"And never we. Soot says we about everything. Up there it is one. One does not kneel. One is not sorry." She folds her hands. "Try."

"One is..." It does not want to come. "Not sorry."

"Better," says Ilse. "Now mean it."

That part nobody had to teach me.

You can hear what she did in the way I tell this. I have not been able to say *don't* inside my own head for years. It is the one thing they took that I let them keep.

---

**TWO YEARS AFTER THE FURNACE**
*A sealed pump room. Brand.*

The second year. I can walk. I can say granted without tasting it. I still wear the gloves.

Ilse brings a man down the stair. He is fifty, with a face that has been left out in the weather and a halberd he carries the way other men carry an arm. He looks at me for a long time, at my height and my gold eyes and my gloves, and he does not say what he sees.

He was a Warden, Ilse tells me. He deserted, when they ordered him to put a halberd through a child for humming.

His name is Brand.

"Ilse is teaching you to pass," he says. "I am teaching you to live." He sets the halberd against the wall. "Every heir you meet has been handed a blade since he was six and has never once been hit. You have been hit all your breath. That is the only thing you have that they do not."

He takes a practice blade off the rack and throws it to me.

"I am going to swing at your head," says Brand. "Do not stop it. You cannot. Step into it and let it go past, and be standing behind me when it lands. They call it the Turn." He picks up a second blade. "I call it not being where the halberd is."

He swings.

I try to stop it. I am on the floor.

"Where were your feet?" says Brand.

I get up.

He swings again.

---

**TWO AND A HALF YEARS AFTER THE FURNACE**
*The pit. Brand. No gloves.*

"Take them off," says Brand.

"Ilse said a year."

"Ilse is teaching you to pass." He does not look away. "Take them off."

I take them off. The grip of the blade sits on the old burns as if it were cut for them.

He swings. I step into it. I let it go past, and for the first time I am standing behind him when it lands, and the floor is in front of him and not in front of me.

He turns round slowly, and he looks at my palms. He looks at them the way a man looks at a tool he sold once. He knows what a valve burn is. He has stood guard over the people who got them.

"I hit a welder from behind once," says Brand, "for humming. He did not turn round. He finished the bar he was on." He picks the halberd up off the wall. "I have thought about that bar every day since."

I know then where I have seen his face. Over a halberd, in Bay Three, in orange light. I do not hit him. I want to. I stand there with the practice blade in my bare hand and want to more than I have ever wanted anything, and I do not.

He sees it. He waits.

"When you get up there, boy," says Brand, "do not let them teach you it was ever a fair fight. It was never a fight." He sets the halberd down again, very carefully. "That is what the halberds are for."

---

*Three years of that.*

*Then they sent him up Lift Zero under a dead boy's name, into the Assay.*

---

**FOUR YEARS AFTER THE FURNACE**
*The Assay. The first night.*

There are more than five hundred of us in the lift, packed shoulder to shoulder in white, and it climbs for longer than I have ever climbed anything.

The boy beside me is sixteen. He is the youngest one there. He has a heel of bread in his pack, and halfway up he breaks it down the middle and holds out the larger piece.

"One ought to go up on something," he says.

His name is Dorian Aldane. He asks about my family. I lie well.

The lift opens on grass.

I have read the word. I stand on it for a full minute before I can make myself walk.

An Assayer reads out a dead boy's name, and I answer to it. Edric Sorrel, of a minor House with no money to bury him loudly, dead of a fever at eleven. Ilse bought his papers. Nobody looks twice. She was right about that. They do not look at each other, only at what the other is worth.

Every House keeps four seats for heirs nobody has paid for. There are eight of us for every House, and that night they seal the ones nobody paid for in by twos.

Two go in. One comes out a cadet.

---

The room is stone, with a door at each end and a gallery above, where the Assayers stand with their slates. There is a blade on the floor for each of us.

The door I came in by closes.

Dorian picks up his blade. He is not bad at this. Somebody loved him enough to pay for lessons. He looks at me across the room with his clean young face, and I see him understand who he has been put in with, and I see him decide to try anyway.

"Who is this for?" he says.

I do not answer. Up in the gallery they are keeping score.

I will not write the rest of it. I said her name until it was the only thing in the room. He stopped trying to win and only tried to last. At the end he was on the floor, and he looked up at me, and his voice was very quiet.

"I hope she was worth it," said Dorian Aldane.

I had never told him there was a she.

The other door opened. They took him out through it.

I count him. I count all of them. He is not one of the eleven. He has a number of his own.

---

**MORNING**
*The Assay. Day three. The camp by daylight.*

I wake with my hand on half a loaf of bread, and for a moment he is still holding out the other half.

Forty of us in one House, and every one of them was sent by somebody.

House Violet's camp is a ring of tents by a river in a valley so green it hurts. There is a wood full of deer. There is a fire ring of laid stones, and it is cold. Nobody has eaten in three days.

At the edge of the camp the Assayers have hammered in a board. Twelve bars, one for each House, repainted from the air each dawn. Violet is eleventh. Under the bars, in small clean script: POSITIONS ARE PROVISIONAL. DEBTS ARE NOT.

Below, the board is the shift rota, and the bars are us.

Petra Lune keeps the House roll on a slate. Forty names, a column for bread, a column for water, and a third she has not headed yet. Below, we would have headed it OWED. At the foot of her slate, apart from the forty, there is one more name with a box drawn round it. *Aldane, D.* She has not written what became of him. Nobody has told her. Nobody has told me, and I was in the room.

A cadet who has not slept is sitting outside her tent, watching the far side of the valley.

"They took the losers out by the other door," she says, to nobody. "One watched for it to open again. It has not opened again."

Nobody up here says where that door goes. They say a pairing *balanced*. I have heard the word used that way before.

Corin Ashby has counted the lamp oil. He has a slate and a clerk's hand, neat sevens.

"Twelve flasks," he says. "For forty, for a season. It is only arithmetic, Sorrel, but somebody ought to be doing it."

He is right. He is also pleased to be right. I do not know yet which of those is going to matter.

Two cadets in teal come up from the river to read our bar. Nobody stops them. They have the same face and the same way of standing, a hand apart. They do not look at me. They look at where I would have to put my feet to reach them both.

Oswin Hale is trying to split wet wood with a practice blade. They are issued blunt, four blades for forty cadets, on purpose. He has not worked out yet that it is on purpose.

"Father finished ninth at his Assay," he says, without looking up. "He has been ninth for thirty years. One is not to finish ninth. That was the whole of the advice."

I sit down by him.

"One had rather hoped nobody would," says Oswin.

We do not talk. I take the blade off him and show him where the grain runs. Below, a child knows that. He watches my hands and not the wood, and he sees the burns on my palms, and he says nothing about what he sees.

---

**NIGHT THREE**
*The Assay. Day three, after dark.*

There is wood. Somebody cut it before the Assay began and stacked it where the cadets would find it. They have walked past it for three days.

One of them is sitting on it.

Cassel Thorne is tall and clean and was elected captain of House Violet on the lift, by acclaim. He has had a fencing master since he was six. Nobody has ever hit him in the mouth.

"Hands off, Sorrel," he says. "A minor House does not help itself. One was elected captain. One will say when there is to be a fire."

"You don't know how to make one," I say.

It comes out in the wrong voice, the old one. Nobody notices but me.

Thorne stands up off the woodpile. His hand goes to his blade. Then he looks round at the forty of them, watching, and lets go of it.

"Blades down," he says. "The Assayers mark a drawn blade inside one's own camp. I shall do this the other way."

He takes off his cloak and folds it.

It is short. He hits like a lesson: neat, with nothing behind it. I hit like Bank Seven. At the end he is sitting in the grass with his lip split, learning what his own blood tastes like, and not caring for it.

"House Violet," says Petra Lune, very dry, "appears to have a new captain. He is the one bleeding less."

---

The pump house stands at the water's edge, the only thing in the valley built like home: dressed stone, a wheel, a gauge. Names are cut into the lintel, a row for every Assay. Hundreds of them. A few have a small gold nail beside them. Most have a line scored through.

They keep a Count even here.

My hand is flat on the pipe before I think about it.

It draws. The water comes up warm.

Warm. There is only one thing under this rock that makes water warm. I know the line. I have bled it.

The Assay valley is the roof of Cinderwell. Their children are playing at war on top of Tier Four, and the pump they cannot work is fed off the same Array my tier is rationed from. I stand with my hand on the pipe and listen. Eleven hundred people, forty feet down.

I cannot hear one of them.

---

I lay the fire the way she taught me.

Flint off the pump housing. A curl of bark. Strike, and strike, and breathe.

It catches.

Forty of them are standing round me in the dark. Nobody says anything for a long moment.

"It is lit," says Petra. Her voice is strange. "He has lit it."

"How did you learn that, Sorrel?" says Oswin.

"One was taught by somebody better."

"Then," says Petra, "I should like to have met them."

*No*, I think. *You would have had her kneel.*

I do not say it. I feed the fire.

They come out of the tents one at a time and sit down. Forty of them. Nobody gives an order. By morning they are asking me where to put the pickets.

I have thought about it since. They did not follow me because I walked out of the Pairing, or because I put Thorne on the grass. They followed me because I could light a fire.

It is the cheapest thing I ever sold them.
