# SOOTBORN

## Chapter Two: Last Bell

---

*For three nights, we bled the line and balanced the bank by dawn.*

*On the third night, the fever broke. Tavi asked for bread.*

---

The children called it the storm.

For three nights the ward fan ran so fast that the paper on Nell's board lifted at the corners, and the children lay in their cots with their mouths shut and breathed through their noses for the first time in a month, and listened to it.

On the first night Nell met me at the door with her arms folded.

"Don't tell me how," she said. "I've forty of them breathing and a meter that says I should have twelve. I'm not going to ask it why."

She did not ask. She chalked 40 on the board and underlined it, and then she sat down on the floor, which I had never seen her do.

Seren stood beside me in the doorway and listened to the fan.

"Listen to that," she said. "That's not a fan, that's a gale. You've given them weather, valve man."

"Eleven in the hundred, off a line nobody reads. It balances by dawn."

"It'd better. I'm not married to a thief." She leaned on my arm. "I'm married to a man who tidies."

The High Auditor used almost the same words three nights later, in the square. I have never been able to hear the one without the other.

On the third night Tavi sat up in his cot and put his hand on his chest and looked at me with his eyes very round.

"I can breathe right down to the bottom," he said. "Kael. Right to the bottom. Is it always like this?"

It had not been like that for anybody on Tier Six in his lifetime. I said aye.

In the morning he asked for bread.

---

*On the fourth morning, the audit came early.*

*It did not come by gauge. It came down Lift Zero.*

---

The whole tier was summoned to Ashgate. Nobody was told why. Nobody needed to be.

We stood in rows in front of the Pillar, crew by crew, the way we stood for everything. Seren was beside me in Crew Three's line. Her shoulder was against mine. She did not say anything, and neither did I, and her hand found mine between us where nobody could see and held on to it very hard.

Lift Zero opened for the first time in eleven years.

Light came out of it like water out of a broken main. Real light. Not Pillar light. It had a color none of us had a word for.

They came down in it. Tall and unhurried, robed in a white that had never known a smudge. Their skin had never known soot. Their voices had never needed to be raised. Standing near them felt like standing near the sun Seren had painted on the Pillar. You could not look straight at them. You could not look away.

A grey man came out in front of them with a notebook in his hand and a silver pin on his collar. Inspector Vale. He had been born in Cinderwell, people said, on Tier Four, and counted up. You would not have known it from his face.

"Kneel," said Vale, "for High Auditor Celestine Aurel of the Array Authority."

Cinderwell knelt. It is very good at kneeling.

She was smaller than I had imagined. I had seen her on the screens, on the posters, under the gold sun with its open eye. Here she was a woman in white gloves with her hair pinned flat and a face as clean as a new page in a ledger. She walked into Ashgate Square as if she had been there before and found it smelt of exactly what she had expected.

"The Cinders," said the High Auditor. "How... warm it is down here. I had been told. One is never quite prepared for the smell."

Nobody moved.

"For three nights," she said, "a line that belongs to the Authority lost eleven percent of its pressure. And for three dawns, someone put it back. Very neatly." She turned her head, slowly, along the front row. "I confess I admire it. A thief who tidies. It is almost civilized."

My heart was a hammer. My weight went to my feet.

"But the account must balance," said Celestine Aurel. "And an account is not balanced by putting air back. It is balanced by taking something back."

Vale did not look up from his notebook. "The bleed was routed through Valve Bank Seven. Crew Three. Night shift."

Mara felt it before I did. Her hand closed on the back of my collar from the row behind.

"Don't," she breathed. "Kael. Don't you dare."

Seren let go of my hand.

She stood up.

She did it slowly, the way she did everything she wanted people to see, and she walked out of Crew Three's line into the open space in front of the High Auditor with her chin up and her hands at her sides.

"I turned the valve," said Seren Holloway. "Alone. Every night."

Every head in the square turned. She did not look at me. She was very, very careful not to look at me.

It was a lie. I had turned the valve. She had kept the door. She had stood in it with a sack of bolts and sworn at a Tallyman so that he would look at her and not at me, and now she was standing in the square doing the same thing again.

The High Auditor looked at her with mild interest.

"Oh, look," she said. "The soot has a spine." She tilted her head. "Your name, little ember?"

"Seren Holloway. Crew Three." Seren's voice did not shake at all. "Forty kids are breathing because of me. I'd do it again tonight."

Celestine Aurel considered this.

"There are always forty children," she said. "That is rather the problem with you."

I was getting up. I did not decide to. My knees were off the stones and Mara had both hands in my collar and her mouth against my ear.

"She's already chosen, boy," Mara said. "Don't you make it for nothing."

I stayed down. I have counted that, since, among the things I did. I stayed down.

"Seren Holloway of Crew Three," said the High Auditor. "For theft from the Array, you are committed to the Array. Your breath returns to the covenant." She was already turning back toward the light. "The heart-furnace, Bay Three, at last bell. Crew Three will attend. Gratitude is best learned up close."

---

**LAST BELL**
*Bay Three. The heart-furnace.*

They did not do it in the dark. The Authority believes in witnesses.

All of Crew Three stood in the front row. Behind us, the tier: every crew, every family, children on shoulders. Nell with her hands folded. Ivo with his face wet. Hollis the welder at the end of our row, humming under his breath the way he always did, because twelve hours on a wheel puts the tune in your jaw and it does not come out. Mara on my left, with her hand on my shoulder, holding me in the row.

Tavi was on my right. They had made me bring him. Tavi too. Especially Tavi.

"Tav," I said. "Don't look. Look at me. Look at my boots."

"I'm looking at her," said my brother. "Somebody has to."

The heart-furnace was a black drum the height of two men, with doors in the front the size of a cart, and it breathed. You could feel it breathe on your face from twenty paces. Its light came out round the seam of the doors in a slow orange pulse, and every pulse swung the shadows in the bay toward it and away.

The High Auditor stood on the step beside it, in her gloves, with her hands folded.

"Gratitude, Cinderwell," she said, "is not a feeling. It is a practice. One practises."

Somewhere in the back row, somebody started to hum.

The shift song has no words, because words cost breath. You hum it through your teeth to keep time on a valve wheel, forty people turning as one. Humming is the only goodbye we are allowed. Speaking at a commitment is forbidden. Nobody ever wrote a rule about humming.

Then another started. Then all of Crew Three, low, through closed teeth, one note dropped and climbing.

A Warden walked down our row. I did not know his name then. I learned it in a sealed pump room two years on, from the man himself. He was tall, with a halberd the length of a pipe and a face that looked as if it had been left out in the weather, and he stopped in front of Hollis.

He drove the butt of the halberd into Hollis's arm.

Hollis folded without a sound. He went down on one knee with the arm held against his body, and he did not turn round, and he did not look up. He finished the bar he was on.

The humming stopped. The silence after it was worse.

The Wardens hauled the furnace doors open.

The heat hit the back row like a slap. The whole bay turned the color of a wound.

They brought her in from the side. They had put her in a grey shift with no band on it. Her boots scraped on the grating. She was not crying. I hated myself, later, for being proud of that.

She stopped at the edge of the light and turned and found me across the bay.

"Hey," she said. "Hey, Kael. Look at me. Not at them. At me."

I looked.

"You were always too much for this place," said Seren. "Too loud, too proud, too much." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "Good."

I could not breathe.

"Be too much for *them*."

She lifted her hand so that I could see the brass washer on her finger, catching the fire.

"Balance the bank," said my wife. "And don't you *dare* stop breathing."

I held her eyes. I did not scream. I did not run. Mara's hand was on my shoulder and I did not need it. I held her eyes across Bay Three so that she would not be alone in the light, and she held mine, and then she turned round.

She walked the last of it herself. They did not have to push her. She would not give them that.

The doors began to close.

**ONE.**
*She steals my bread on the first day of school and denies it with her mouth full.*

**TWO.**
*Fifteen. Behind the noodle stall. She kisses me first, then tells the whole tier I started it.*

**THREE.**
*She paints her first sun on our bunk wall. My mother pretends to be angry for a week.*

**FOUR.**
*The Tier Eight cave-in. She digs me out with her bare hands. She never once mentions it again.*

**FIVE.**
*"Kael Brennick, you are the most arrogant man in the Cinders." "Top three." "Top one."*

**SIX.**
*The washer. Three weeks of lunch breaks. Her face when she sees it.*

**SEVEN.**
*Pressure-bound at the Pillar. Ivo sobbing. Chalk on both our hands.*

**EIGHT.**
*Tavi's fever. Her hand flat on my back, all night, so I would remember to breathe.*

**NINE.**
*The clinic fans spinning up. Her laughing into my collar.*

**TEN.**
*"Be too much for them."*

**ELEVEN.**

---

The doors shut.

"Balanced," said the High Auditor.

Ash came down from the furnace vents. It landed in my hair. On my hands. I did not brush it off. I have never brushed it off.

She came down the step. She did not hurry. She walked along the front row of Crew Three the way you walk along a shelf, and she stopped in front of me.

"You," she said. "The loud one."

I looked at her.

"Grief is a luxury item, little soot," said Celestine Aurel. "You will be invoiced."

I memorized her. The exact gold of her eyes. The way she stood, as if nothing she had ever carried had been heavy. The smell of her, like rain I had never stood in.

"I'll see you again," I said.

She was already turning away. She did not stop.

"Everyone does, eventually," she said. "From below."

---

I do not know how long I stayed on the grating. Long enough for the bay to empty. Long enough for the furnace to tick down.

Mara's hand came back to my shoulder.

"Kael," she said. "Kael, son. Get up. She'd want you up."

I looked at the doors.

"She told me to breathe," I said.

Mara waited.

"So every breath I take now is hers." I got up. "And every breath *they* take is mine."

Something in me went quiet then, and stayed quiet. Something else woke up, and did not go back to sleep.

---

Two days later, a tin came to the door of K-7. Authority grey, with a little sun stamped on the lid. Somebody up there thought that was a nice touch.

Inside: a cup of ash. Less than you would think. And a brass washer, blackened, still round.

And an invoice.

COMMITMENT FUEL — 14 SCRIP, DEDUCTED FROM FUTURE WAGES. GRATITUDE IS BREATH.

They returned the ring. They kept the girl. And they sent me the bill for the fire.
