# SOOTBORN: Chapter One
*Be too much for them.*

---

**CINDERWELL**
*Six tiers under the rock. Before any of it.*

Above us the sky is a gold you are not allowed to look at. Down here it is a ceiling of pipe.

Six tiers stacked on each other like a bad shelf, and every one of them breathes through the same few drums at the bottom. They tell us the air comes down from the sky. They tell us the sky is the only place a person can live, and that we should be grateful to be allowed to live under it at all.

I have never seen the sky. I have a pipe.

I was born on Tier Six. I work Tier Six. I will die on it unless somebody counts me out, and the Array Authority keeps the count. Every breath down here is logged, rationed and owed. There is a board in every bay that says so, in letters a hand tall, under a gold sun with an eye in it.

GRATITUDE IS BREATH.

I have never been able to work out whose breath it is we are grateful for.

---

**ELEVEN MONTHS BEFORE THE FURNACE**
*The Festival of the First Fire. Ashgate Square.*

Seren Holloway painted suns on everything. The walls of the stairwell, the lid of the water tank, the back of the Proctor's chair when he was looking at the other way. Every sun had a face in it, and every face was a little bit cross.

She was up a crate on the Pillar when I got to the square, with her sleeves rolled and a tin of gold in one hand.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm early. You're high."

"I'm exactly high enough. Stand still."

I stood still. I had chalk on my palms from the Ember Ring, where Ivo had lost a bet to a Garran brother, and a brass washer in my pocket that I had been turning over since the first bell. It was the one round thing I owned. Ivo had taken a shift band off a dead man's hook for the ring, and cut the middle out of it, and I had taken the middle. It was too small for anything but a finger. I had been practising saying that I was going to put it on hers.

She came down off the crate. She did not look at the washer. She looked at my hands.

"Chalk," she said.

"I know."

"You're going to have it on you when they ask."

"Let them ask."

Mara Venn stood at the foot of the Pillar with her arms folded. She had married half the tier and buried a quarter of it. She did not look like she was enjoying herself.

"Kael Brennick," she said. "Are you asking, or are you standing there?"

"Asking."

"Then ask. She's cold."

I got down on one knee on the hot stones, which I should not have done, and I held out the washer on my open palm.

"Seren Holloway," I said. "I can't give you the sky."

"No," said Seren. "You can't."

"But I can give you a perfectly balanced pressure seal. Every morning, for the rest of your life. Nobody else on this tier can say that."

There was a noise behind me. It was Ivo, crying into a sleeve, and pretending he was coughing.

"That is the worst proposal," said Seren, very clearly, "in the history of Cinderwell."

"Aye," I said.

"Obviously aye." She took the washer off my hand and held it to the light from the Pillar. Then she put it on me, on the third finger of my left hand, where it sat like a ring that had been waiting. "Put it on me before I come to my senses. Say it, then."

"Pressure-bound," said Mara. "Crew Three, you saw it. Now get off the stones before the Proctor sees you kneeling and writes something down."

The tier cheered, which it did not usually do. Tavi, my brother, who was six and had been put on Mara's shoulders for the view, shouted that the sun was on everything, and was right. The Pillar was lit. The square smelled of hot wax and fried dough and the cold blue water they gave out at the pump, and everybody on the tier who could leave their shift had come to see a valve hand get married.

I remember thinking that was the best day I had ever had. I still think it.

---

**FOUR DAYS BEFORE THE FURNACE**
*The fever comes to Tier Six.*

Tavi was my brother. He was seven, and when he breathed it sounded like a sock being pulled over a boot.

I heard it from the bunk. Our bunk, the top one in the back room of Row K, with Seren's paintings all over the wall above it. There were seventeen suns in that room. Tavi counted them every night in a whisper until he fell asleep, and every night he got a different number, and every night he said that was because she had been painting more.

That night he didn't count. He just breathed, like a sock.

"Kael," said Seren, from the floor. She had not slept either. "Get up."

The clinic was on the far side of the tier, behind the old tunnel door and past the water. Nell ran it. She had been a nurse before the Authority decided that a nurse was a luxury item, and she still had the nurse's way of standing with her hands folded so that you could not see her shaking. She was shaking.

There were forty cots in the fever ward. There were forty children in them. Every one of them was breathing with their mouths open, and every one of them had a wet, ugly sound at the bottom of the breath, like a drum with water in it.

"It isn't the fever that kills them," said Nell. "I want you to know that. It's the air."

"I know."

"The Authority cut the clinic line to ration pressure two weeks ago. The vents are open at a quarter. Every meter I have says I should have twelve cubic metres of breath for this room, and I've got three." She looked at Tavi, who was on the cot at the end, with his hand on his chest, and then back at me. "Forty kids. Breathing each other's breath."

I looked at the vent. It was turning slowly, the way a thing does when it has not been asked to turn at all.

"I can fix the vent," I said.

"You can't fix the Array."

"I can fix one pipe."

Nell did not ask which pipe. She knew what I was. A valve hand is a man who knows where the air goes, and where it does not, and who is paid to make sure it goes the right way and nowhere else. Everyone on the tier knew what I was. That is why they had asked me.

There was a line on Bank Seven.

I had read its gauge once, on a shift when I was sixteen and too bored to count the bolts. It was on the north wall of Bay Three, a fat grey pipe with an Authority seal on the flange. The needle sat in the red at all times. It was never going to move, and nobody was ever going to ask why it did not. It carried more air than all of Tier Six put together, every night, somewhere far away and up.

"I'm not going to take the air," I said to Seren. "I'm going to bleed it. A sliver. Into the clinic line. Then we balance the bank again before the morning audit, and nobody sees a thing."

"That's stealing."

"That's a sum."

"That's a sum with forty kids at the bottom of it, Kael."

"Aye," I said. "That's why I'm doing it."

She didn't answer for a while. Then she went to the cupboard, took out her coat and the tin of gold, and put them on the bench where we could both see them.

"Then I'm watching the door," she said.

---

*For three nights, you bled the line and balanced the bank by dawn.*

*On the third night, the fever broke. Tavi asked for bread.*

---

**THE HEIST**

The audit relay sat on the east wall of Bay Three, straight across from the door. A grey box with a red lamp. Cut that first, Seren had said, or the bank logs every turn you make.

The Night Proctor walked the bay every hour with a lantern. He walked the middle, end to end, and looked up and down at each end the way a man checks a stove he is not sure he turned off. Then a second lamp walked the centre column and looked left and right. And a Night Watchman stood by the relay with his back to it, turning all the way round, slow and bored, the way men do when they have been told nobody comes down this far.

The lamps swept. I counted them. Four, six, eight. The gap was always there, if you were patient, and nobody on Tier Six has ever been as patient as a valve hand.

I got down behind the crates. I put my hand on the relay's housing and held it there, two seconds, the way I had been taught, and I did not breathe while the cutter bit into the seal. The lamp came round. It stopped on the crate beside my face.

Then it stopped on Seren.

She was standing in the doorway with a sack of bolts over one shoulder, and she was swearing, loudly, in the voice a runner uses when she has dropped something heavy on her foot.

The Night Proctor turned his lantern towards her. She looked up, a little drunk with the lateness of the hour, and said something I will not repeat, and laughed at it.

The lantern stayed on her for a long time. Then it went away, and the Proctor walked on down the centre of the bay, grumbling, and Seren walked on out of the door with her sack of bolts, as if she had never been there at all.

The relay was cut. The bank was waiting. Valve Bank Seven came up under my hands with its drums singing and its gauges fat with air that was not theirs any more.

I bled one sliver into the clinic line. I balanced the bank by dawn. It was the easiest thing I had ever done, and I have since counted every breath I took that night and found it was the only one that was entirely mine.

---

*On the fourth morning, the audit came early.*

*It did not come by gauge. It came down Lift Zero.*

---

**THE AUDIT**
*Ashgate Square. First bell.*

The lift car came down the shaft like a dropped coin, and it stopped with a sound that made the whole tier look up. The doors opened. Two Proctors stepped out first, grey coats and silver pins, boots ringing on the stone. Then an Inspector, Vale, a thin man with a notebook that he held the way a priest holds a book he has been told not to read aloud.

Then the High Auditor.

She was smaller than I had imagined. I had seen her on the screens, and on the posters, and on the gold sun over the Pillar, where she looked six storeys tall. Here she was a woman in grey gloves, with grey hair pinned flat and a face like a clean ledger. She walked into Ashgate Square as if she had been there before and found it smelt of exactly what she had expected.

"Kneel," said the Inspector, to the square.

The square knelt. The shift song was in every mouth, not sung, only hummed through the teeth, the way we do when we are told to be quiet and cannot manage it.

"The Cinders," said High Auditor Celestine Aurel. "How warm it is down here. I had been told. One is never quite prepared for the smell."

Vale read from his notebook without looking at it. "The bleed was routed through Valve Bank Seven. Crew Three. Night shift."

"Yes," said the Auditor. "For three nights, a line that belongs to the Authority lost eleven percent of its pressure. And for three dawns, someone put it back. Very neatly." She turned her head, slowly, and looked along the front row of kneeling soot, and then she was looking at me. "I confess I admire it. A thief who tidies. It is almost civilised."

Nobody moved.

"But the account must balance," she said. "And an account is not balanced by putting air back. It is balanced by taking something back."

Seren stood up.

She did it slowly, the way she did everything when she wanted people to see it, and she came out of the row and stood in the open space in front of the Auditor, with her chin up and her hands at her sides. She still had chalk on her fingers from the night before.

"It was me," said Seren Holloway.

"Kael—"

"Shut it." She did not turn round. "I turned the valve. Alone. Every night."

The Auditor took off one glove. Only one. She did not look at the palm. She looked at Seren with a sort of tired curiosity, the way a person looks at a clock that has stopped at a time they remember.

"Your name, little ember?"

"Seren Holloway. Crew Three. Forty kids are breathing because of me." Seren's voice did not shake at all. "I'd do it again tonight."

The Auditor put her glove back on.

"There are always forty children," she said, quite gently, to nobody in particular. "That is rather the problem with you."

Then, to Vale: "Seren Holloway of Crew Three. For theft from the Array, she is committed to the Array. Her breath returns to the covenant."

"Aye—" I started, and Mara had me by the back of the collar before I was up.

"The heart-furnace," said the Auditor. "Bay Three, at last bell. Crew Three will attend. Gratitude is best learned up close."

---

**LAST BELL**
*Bay Three. The heart-furnace.*

At last bell the whole tier walked into Bay Three and stood in rows.

Crew Three was at the front, and Crew Three was all of us, every valve hand and runner and sealer who had ever touched Bank Seven, and the families behind them, and the children on their parents' shoulders. Nell was there with her hands folded. Ivo was there with his face wet and nothing to say. Hollis the welder stood at the end of the row with his good arm around his daughter, and Mara was on my left with her hand on my shoulder, holding me in the row the way you hold a bucket you are not sure is going to stay full.

Tavi was on my right. Mara had made me bring him. "He's to see it," she'd said, in a voice I had not heard her use since Seren's mother died. "If he doesn't see it now, he'll see it in a dream his whole life."

The heart-furnace was a black drum the height of two men, with a door in the front the size of a cart. It breathed. You could feel it breathing on your face from twenty paces. Its light was orange and it came out of the door in a slow pulse, and every pulse made the bay's shadows swing towards it, then away.

The Warden stood beside the door. His name was Brand, and he had a halberd with a long steel head, and a face that looked like it had been left out in the weather for a long time.

The shift song started. It was hummed, not sung. One note, dropped and climbing, like a wheel being turned by a man who has done it all his life. Crew Three hummed, and the tier hummed, and the sound filled the bay until the furnace seemed to be humming too.

Then Hollis hummed louder.

He was not doing it to make a point. He was just a welder, with a bad shoulder and a daughter who knew the song, and he was doing what the tier did at every bell. The Warden turned his head.

Brand walked down the row. He did not hurry. He stopped in front of Hollis and looked at him for a long time, and then he swung the butt of the halberd into Hollis's arm, once, with a sound like a wet branch breaking.

Hollis did not cry out. He stopped humming. He went down on one knee with his arm held against his body, and he looked up at the Warden with the face of a man who has finished the bar he was on.

Tavi made a small sound, and I put my hand over his mouth.

Seren was at the furnace door.

They had put her in a grey shift with no band on it. She had her hair up, and no chalk on her fingers any more. She looked at me across the bay and her eyes were perfectly steady. Then she looked at Tavi, and then at the tier, and then at the Auditor, who was standing on the raised step beside the door, in her gloves, with her hands folded.

"Hey," Seren said, to me. Not loudly. Just across the bay, as if we were in the bunk. "Look at me. Not at them. At me."

I looked.

"You were always too much for this place," she said. "Too loud, too proud, too much. Good."

My throat would not work.

"Be too much for THEM," said Seren. "Balance the bank. And don't you DARE stop breathing."

The Warden put his hand on the door.

The furnace opened. The light came out of it all at once, orange and white, and the whole bay went red. The heat hit us like a wall. I heard someone scream, and I think it was Ivo.

Seren walked in.

She did not run. She did not look back. She stepped up to the door and into the light, and the door began to close behind her, slowly, on its great black hinges.

Then the count started.

It was not spoken. It was in my head, the way the count always is, and it went as the door came down. One.

I saw the washer, on a bit of wire, on the bench in the back room of Row K.

Two. The chalk sun on the wall of the stairwell, the face in it a little bit cross.

Three. Ivo's hand on my shoulder at the Pillar, too hard.

Four. Tavi's bunk, the sock sound, the seventeen suns.

Five. The water, cold and blue, out of the pump at the square.

Six. Mara's voice saying, Kneel.

Seven. The grey box on the east wall with its red lamp.

Eight. The lamp on the crate beside my face, and Seren's laugh.

Nine. The gauge on Bank Seven, sitting in the red, not moving.

Ten. The Auditor's glove going back on.

Eleven.

The door shut.

It was black in the bay for a moment, black and silent, the way a room is when someone has just gone out of it. Nobody hummed. Nobody breathed. In the silence I heard the furnace settle on its hinges, like a beast turning over in its sleep.

"Balanced," said the High Auditor, from the step.

Mara's hand was still on my shoulder. I did not know how long it had been there.

I counted the eleven again, on my breath, very slowly, so that I would not lose one. I have counted them every day since.
