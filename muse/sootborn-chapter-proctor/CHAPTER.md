# SOOTBORN: Nobody Touches a Proctor
*A high-energy chapter. Day thirty-one of the Assay.*

---

**THE ASSAY**
*Day thirty-one. He has noticed the page.*

The storm comes up the valley like a lift car dropped down a shaft.

Rain, sideways. Wind that tastes of iron. Seventy cadets in the mud of the camp, Violet and Teal together, which has never happened, because Teal came over to us nine days ago with a standard on a shoulder and has not found a way to leave. Nobody is cooking. Nobody is talking. Everybody is looking up.

Above the ridge, the white.

Proctors hang there. They have hung there for thirty days, humming on their boots, writing down every cut we make. From the ground they look like small clean gods. From the ground you cannot see their faces.

One of them is coming down.

I know which one before he is close. I know the way he drops: straight, unhurried, like a man stepping off a stair he has never once had to climb. Halden Voss. Two patrons, one of them Teal. A column in the grading book with a price beside every name. Mine has two words after it.

RECOMMEND REMOVAL.

I tore that page out last night and folded it into my collar, next to the washer, and I have been waiting since for him to come and ask for it.

He lands in front of the fire. The fire hisses. Nobody breathes.

"Cadet Sorrel."

"Proctor."

"A page has gone from the roster." He is tall, with a clerk's neat hands and a face that has never been hit by anything it could see coming. "One would have it back."

"One does not have it."

"Then one will have you." The halberd in his hand is not for show. It is the length of a pipe, with a butt that has been in the same place between a great many shoulders. "Come. Quietly. The Assay does not need to see this."

Seventy people are watching, and he has just told me the Assay does not need to.

I take one step.

---

There is a rule. There is exactly one rule in this valley that the Proctors enforce, and it is this one.

*Nobody touches a Proctor.*

Not a hand. Not a blade. Not a breath on his collar. Everything else, they let us do to each other, and they write it down, and they put a price beside it. This one thing is different. You touch a Proctor and you are not a cadet. You are a line struck through.

I know. I have read the grading book.

"Sorrel." Petra's voice, low, behind me. "One does not."

"One knows."

Oswin is standing on his bad leg, the right, and he does not look at it, and he does not tell me to stop. He only looks at me the way you look at a valve you have been told is sound and have your doubts about.

I think about a pipe under a floor. I think about her hand, flat on it. *Hands, Kael. Everything is hands.*

I think: forty beds.

"You cannot," says Halden Voss, because he has seen my face and he is a clever man. "There is no line for this. There is no column. Nobody has ever needed one."

"Then one will have to be added," I say.

And I go.

---

He is already rising.

Boots. That is the whole of it, that is what a Proctor *is*: a man, and a pair of boots that hum, and a halberd, and a long way up. He gets six feet off the mud before I have crossed the fire. Ten. His halberd comes round in a low arc that would take my legs off at the knee if I were where he thinks I am.

I am not.

I have long legs now. They cost nine months and a spool of gold wire, and for two years I hated them, and today I love them. I go up off the woodpile like something fired out of a pipe, and I have the first breath of him in my teeth, rain and cold metal and fresh linen, and then I am *over* the arc, and the halberd goes under me, and he looks up.

*Look at that. He has to look up.*

"Cadet," says Voss. It comes out thin.

I am at the top of my jump. There is nothing under me. He has made sure of that; that is what the boots are for.

*Jump, then jump again.*

Brand never taught me this one. I came up a lift shaft. I know what it is to be in the air with no floor. I kick off the haft of his own halberd, off the thing he is holding, *off him,* and I go up again, past his shoulder, into the rain, and the whole valley turns white and gold under me, and somebody in the camp makes a sound like a pipe splitting.

Then I come down.

I come down the way a shaft drop comes: all of it, all at once, straight through the place he is standing. My blade is the bank hook. Their steel, our hook on the end, and it knows exactly where to go, because it was made for the one thing a valve hand does that nobody above ever has to: *cutting a seat out of something that will not let go.*

The straps on his left boot part like rotten belting.

The right.

Halden Voss drops out of the sky like a coat off a hook.

---

He lands in the mud on his back, and the mud goes up around him in a wave, and a hum that has been in the air for thirty days *stops.*

Silence. Rain. Seventy people not breathing.

I am standing on his chest.

No. I am standing next to him, with my boots on the ground. I think I was on his chest. It does not matter. The straps are cut. The boots are in the mud like two dead birds. He is on the ground, in the camp, in the rain, among the rest of us.

He does not know how to stand in mud.

He gets up on one knee, and I watch him learn what every cadet in this valley has been learning for thirty days, which is that *down here, it is slippery.* He has not touched the ground since the Assay began. His halberd is still in his hand. His face has gone the color of the inside of a lamp.

"You will be unmade for this," he says.

"Granted."

He swings.

---

A halberd butt, between the shoulders. The same place. Every time.

I know it before I see it. Every bone in me knows it: the way it comes out of nowhere, the way it lands like a door. I have had it in the back of the knees on a tier six floor. I have had it in my ribs in a square. I have had it in my arm. I have watched it put a welder down for humming.

*Where were your feet?*

Brand's voice. Right there, behind my ear, like he is standing in the rain with me.

*An heir would be dead.*

I am not an heir.

I step into it.

I step *into* the swing, and the butt goes past my head, close enough that I feel the wind of it in my gilded eyes, and I am behind him. The way you are behind a halberd when the halberd has not been told it is a valve. *You do not stop pressure. You send it somewhere.* He sails past with all that weight behind the swing and finds nothing in front of him.

I put my hand flat between his shoulders.

Not a blow. A hand.

He goes down on his face.

Somebody in the camp says, very quietly, in a voice that has never said it before: "*Oh.*"

---

It starts then. I feel it start. It starts the way it started in a bay with the furnace doors opening, in the base of the spine, a pressure behind the ribs like a seal about to lift. Fury. Not anger. Anger is for equals, she told me, a woman I would very much like to say something to. Fury is a bar that fills, and it is full, and I am holding my breath.

*One.*

I hit him. Not with the blade. With the hand, in the shoulder, the way you strike a seized wheel.

*Two.*

He is a clerk's son and a Proctor, and he has never in his life been hit by anything that was not paid for.

*Three. Four.*

I hit him for the doors. I hit him for the Hadleys' ration book. I hit him for forty-one days of grey in a tier's mouth and a tin with less in it than you would think.

*Five. Six. Seven.*

He is bleeding. He looks at his own blood on his palm as if it were a clerical error. He is trying to say something and it is coming out like a hinge.

*Eight.*

I hit him for every man who ever sat behind a gold seal and wrote down my wife's name wrong.

*Nine.* The rain is in my mouth.

*Ten.* He has stopped saying Proctor. He is saying a different word. They have it. I did not think they had it.

"Please," says Halden Voss.

*Eleven.*

I stop.

I stop with my fist an inch from his face, shaking. One for every second the doors stood open. One for every second he would have given me. I hold it there, and I count the last second in my head, and it is the longest second I have ever lived, and at the end of it I open my hand.

He is on the ground in the rain, alive, with my blood on his hands and his on mine, and every cadet in this valley is looking at him.

That is the point. That is *all* the point.

Forty cadets of Violet and thirty of Teal watch a Proctor lie in the mud, and they learn it in one breath. They learn what my tier has never been allowed to learn.

*They fall over like anybody.*

---

I take the boots.

They are lighter than they look. I thought they would be heavy. I have watched them hang over us, humming, for thirty days, and I thought they would be the weight of a decision. They are the weight of two pairs of good shoes, and in my hands they are *dead*. No hum. No lift. Leather and buckles and a plate with a serial number on the heel.

I take the seal off his collar. It is a small gold thing on a cord, and it is warm from his throat.

I take his cloak.

White. Gold at the hem. It is the most beautiful thing I have ever held, and I put it on over the blood of the man who was wearing it, and I turn to the camp, and not one of them makes a sound.

Then I look at the boots, and I think: *what makes them go?*

---

**THE VENT STACK**
*The Assay. Day thirty-one. The island.*

I know the answer. I knew it on night twenty-five, with my hand flat on a pipe and a girl's voice at my back saying *I find I should like to see whether it works.*

The lamps. The pump. The boots. They all run off the same place: the vent stack on the island, thirty-six metres of dressed iron coming up out of the rock in the middle of the river, with a gilt plate on its side that says it all. DRAWN FROM RISER NINE. *Our* air. Forty feet under Tier Four, somebody is turning a wheel so that a Proctor need not touch the ground.

They run off us. They always have.

I go in the storm, because the storm is the only cover there is.

The ford is up to my thighs and ice-cold. The island's pickets are Teal's, the ones that did not come over, and they are watching the water. They do not watch the sky, because nobody does, because nobody up here has ever had to. I go round them on my belly through the reeds, with a dead man's boots tied around my neck and my own blade in my teeth, and I do not think about it. I think about the number.

*Thirty-six.*

The keeper's stair goes up the side of the stack. Iron rungs, wet, slick as an eel. There is a slate tag wired to the rail, CHECKED, a date, two initials. K.D. Somebody up here does the rounds. Somebody up here knows what a seal is, and who keeps it, and has come every nine days for a season and found nothing wrong.

I climb.

Wind. Rain. Thirty-six metres. The valley opens under me with every rung: the river, the camp, the ridge, the dais with its cold white banner. The lamps of House Teal are small and bright. The fire in my own camp is a coal.

At ten metres I stop thinking. At twenty I stop feeling my hands. At thirty I put my face against the iron and the whole stack hums against my cheek, a low note, a sound I have heard my whole life, the note of a thing that is carrying more air than it should.

Thirty-six.

The crown.

A collar of iron, worn bright where a hundred Proctors have stood their heels. A flat place the width of a table. A wind that could take your coat off.

I put the boots on it.

I stand on them.

---

For a long time, nothing.

Then a breath. One long breath, the way a ward fan does when the branch comes up and the air goes down the line. I feel it under my heels, up through the soles of my own boots, into my shins, my knees, the places the wire went in.

They *light*.

Blue. Pale and clean at the edge and very bright at the root, like a flame you could not strike with any match in Cinderwell. The rain goes round it. The whole stack goes round it, a ring of cold blue light on the crown of the vent, and I am standing in the middle of it with a dead boy's name and a white cloak and my hands shaking.

*They told us the boots were what made a Proctor.*

*They are a battery, and a stack, and a man who is not afraid of the height.*

I look down. Thirty-six metres. The whole valley. The wind is trying to take me off the edge.

I have never been afraid of a height in my life. I came up a lift shaft.

I jump.

---

I fall.

Of course I fall. For one second, for two, for the length of a held breath, I fall straight down the side of the stack with the cloak streaming up over my head and the river coming up black and fast and a sound in my own throat that is not a word.

*One. Two. Three.*

I think: *I did not think this through.*

*Four. Five. Six.*

I think of her, and the thing she said, the last thing, with the fire on her face: *don't you DARE stop breathing.*

*Seven. Eight.*

And the boots take it.

I feel them *catch,* like a seal catching on a flange, like a weight coming off a line. The fall turns over in my stomach. The river is a hand's width under me, and the water goes white and flat beneath me, and I am not going down.

I am going *up.*

---

I will not tell you what it is like. I have tried. In the dark of the Assay I have tried to find the word for it, and the only word I have is *warm.* The air under me is warm. It is *our* air. It smells like the cold crate and the ward and the inside of a leaf. The valley is spread out like a shift rota and every light on it is a number and I am *above all of them,* with a Proctor's boots on my feet and a hole where a ceiling should be and rain on my face that tastes of nothing at all.

I laugh.

I do not remember the last time. I think it was a pipe, and a girl who said a sun wants all its rays or it is just a spider.

I laugh and I climb and I come down on the ridge like a man stepping off a stair, and I fall over, and I get up, and I fall over again.

It takes me the rest of that day to stop falling out of the sky.

---

I come down the ridge after dark.

The storm has gone. The camp is a ring of banked fires and sleeping bodies and nobody is sleeping. I am wearing a Proctor's boots and a Proctor's cloak with a Proctor's blood on the hem, and a gold seal on a cord around my neck, and nobody on the dais has come down to take it off me.

*That is the thing. I have been waiting for it all night. Nobody has.*

I walk into the camp.

The cache is at the far end. I knew where it was. I have walked past it every day for thirty days, a white ribbon across the lid and a card on top. NOT TO BE OPENED BY CADETS. A RESULT MUST BE EARNED. Forty paces from seventy hungry children. They would sooner starve than be marked down.

I put the seal against the lock.

It opens.

Bread. Salve. Lamp oil. I take it out with my hands and I give it out one by one, Violet and Teal, in the order they are standing, and nobody says thank you, because they have never been taught it, and nobody says noted, because it is the wrong word. They take it. They eat. A girl in a borrowed cloak weeps into a heel of bread and does not seem to know it.

I give the salve to Oswin, last. He looks at it. He looks at me.

"It was eleven days late," he says, very quietly. "For the leg. The Proctors said. A result must be earned."

"I know."

"Do you think it will set?"

"I think," I say, "that you have been walking on it for five days and nobody has fallen over."

He takes it.

I turn round.

They are all behind me.

I did not call them. I did not give an order. Forty of Violet and thirty of Teal have come up out of the mud and the dark and stood in a line, in the firelight, in the order they happened to be standing, and they are looking at me. At the cloak. At the white.

Not at the House. Not at the name on my papers. *At the cloak.*

I have thought since that this is all a Proctor ever was. A man, and something white to stand in.

I have thought since that it is a great deal more dangerous than a halberd.

"Sorrel," says Petra Lune, and for once she does not say *one.*

I look at her.

"What do we do now?"

I look at the valley, the dais, the island with its blue ring of light going out like a tier at last bell. Forty paces from us a cache stands open and empty, and across the ridge somebody is writing a column about it, and in the morning the Proctors will come down in white, and they will find a man in their cloak, and they will find that nobody has taught them what to do.

I think: *they did not follow me because I won.*

I think: *they followed me because I came back.*

"One," I say, "will think of something."

I hear myself. I hear the *one.* Three years of Ilse, and I will always say it under my breath.

But under it, in the very back of my throat, where the wire never went, a different word comes up, and for the first time in thirty-one days it is *we.*

---

*END OF CHAPTER*
