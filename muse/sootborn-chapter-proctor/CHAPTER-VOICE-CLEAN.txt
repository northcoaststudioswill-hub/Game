SOOTBORN. Nobody Touches a Proctor.

THE ASSAY. Day thirty-one. He has noticed the page.

The storm comes up the valley like a lift car dropped down a shaft.

Rain, sideways. Wind that tastes of iron. Seventy cadets in the mud of the camp, Violet and Teal together, which has never happened. Teal came over to us nine days ago, with a standard on a shoulder, and has not found a way to leave.

Nobody is cooking. Nobody is talking. Everybody is looking up.

Above the ridge, the white.

Proctors hang there. They have hung there for thirty days, humming on their boots, writing down every cut we make. From the ground they look like small, clean gods. From the ground, you cannot see their faces.

One of them is coming down.

I know which one before he is close. I know the way he drops. Straight. Unhurried. A man stepping off a stair he has never once had to climb. Halden Voss. Two patrons, one of them Teal. A column in the grading book with a price beside every name.

Mine has two words after it.

RECOMMEND REMOVAL.

I tore that page out last night and folded it into my collar, beside the washer. I have been waiting ever since for him to come and ask.

He lands in front of the fire. The fire hisses. Nobody breathes.

"Cadet Sorrel."

"Proctor."

"A page has gone from the roster. One would have it back."

"One does not have it."

"Then one will have you. Come. Quietly. The Assay does not need to see this."

Seventy people are watching, and he has just told me the Assay does not need to.

I take one step.

There is a rule. Exactly one rule in this valley that the Proctors enforce, and it is this one.

Nobody touches a Proctor.

Not a hand. Not a blade. Not a breath on his collar. Everything else, they let us do to each other, and they write it down, and put a price beside it. This one thing is different. You touch a Proctor, and you are not a cadet. You are a line, struck through.

I know. I have read the grading book.

"Sorrel. One does not."

"One knows."

Oswin is standing on his bad leg, the right. He does not look at it. He does not tell me to stop. He only looks at me the way you look at a valve you were told is sound.

I think of a pipe under a floor. Her hand, flat on it. Hands, Kael. Everything is hands.

Forty beds.

"You cannot. There is no line for this. There is no column. Nobody has ever needed one."

"Then one will have to be added."

And I go.

He is already rising.

Boots. That is all a Proctor is. A man, a pair of boots that hum, a halberd, and a long way up. Six feet off the mud before I have crossed the fire. Ten. The halberd comes round in a low arc that would take my legs off at the knee if I were where he thinks I am.

I am not.

I have long legs now. They cost nine months and a spool of gold wire. For two years I hated them. Today, I love them.

I go up off the woodpile like something fired out of a pipe. Rain and cold metal and fresh linen in my teeth, and I am over the arc, and the halberd goes under me, and he looks up.

Look at that. He has to look up.

"Cadet."

I am at the top of the jump. Nothing under me. He made sure of that. That is what the boots are for.

Jump, then jump again.

I kick off the haft of his own halberd, off the thing he is holding, off him, and I go up past his shoulder into the rain, and the whole valley turns white and gold under me, and somebody in the camp makes a sound like a pipe splitting.

Then I come down.

I come down the way a shaft drop comes. All of it. All at once. Straight through the place he is standing. My blade is the bank hook, their steel with our end on it, and it knows exactly where to go.

The straps on his left boot part like rotten belting.

The right.

Halden Voss drops out of the sky like a coat off a hook.

He lands in the mud, on his back. The mud goes up in a wave. And a hum that has been in the air for thirty days stops.

Silence. Rain. Seventy people not breathing.

He does not know how to stand in mud.

He gets up on one knee, and I watch him learn what every cadet in this valley has learned in thirty days: down here, it is slippery. His face has gone the color of the inside of a lamp.

"You will be unmade for this."

"Granted."

He swings.

A halberd butt, between the shoulders. The same place. Every time. I know it before I see it. Every bone in me knows it. I have had it in the back of the knees on a Tier Six floor. I have had it in my ribs, in a square. I have watched it put a welder down for humming.

Where were your feet?

Here.

I step into it.

I step into the swing, and the butt goes past my head, close enough that I feel the wind of it, and I am behind him. A halberd that has not been told it is a valve. You do not stop pressure. You send it somewhere.

He sails past, all that weight behind the swing, and finds nothing in front of him.

I put my hand flat between his shoulders. Not a blow. A hand.

He goes down on his face.

"Oh."

It starts then.

It starts the way it started in a bay with the furnace doors opening. In the base of the spine. A pressure behind the ribs, like a seal about to lift. Not anger. Anger is for equals, she told me, and I would very much like to say something to her. This is fury. A bar that fills. And it is full, and I am holding my breath.

One.

I hit him.

Two.

Three. Four.

That is for the doors. That is for the Hadleys' ration book. That is for forty-one days of grey in a whole tier's mouth, and a tin with less in it than you would think!

FIVE! SIX! SEVEN!

He is bleeding. He is looking at his own blood on his palm as if it were a clerical error.

EIGHT! For every man who ever sat behind a gold seal and wrote my wife's name wrong! NINE! TEN!

He has stopped saying Proctor. He is saying a different word. They have it. I did not know they had it.

"Please."

Eleven.

I stop.

My fist is an inch from his face, shaking. One for every second the doors stood open. One for every second he would have given me. I hold it there, and count the last one in my head, and it is the longest second of my life.

At the end of it, I open my hand.

He is on the ground in the rain, alive, with my blood on his hands and his on mine. Every cadet in the valley is looking at him.

That is the point. That is all of the point.

Forty of Violet and thirty of Teal watch a Proctor lie in the mud, and they learn it in one breath. They learn what my tier has never been allowed to learn.

They fall over like anybody.

I take the boots.

They are lighter than they look. I thought they would weigh as much as a decision. They weigh two pairs of good shoes, and in my hands they are dead. No hum. No lift. Leather and buckles and a serial number on the heel.

I take the seal off his collar. A small gold thing on a cord, still warm from his throat.

I take his cloak. White. Gold at the hem. The most beautiful thing I have ever held. I put it on over the blood of the man who wore it, and I turn to the camp, and not one of them makes a sound.

I look at the boots, and I think: what makes them go?

THE VENT STACK. The Assay. Day thirty-one. The island.

I know. I knew on night twenty-five, with my hand flat on a pipe and a girl behind me saying she should like to see whether it works.

The lamps. The pump. The boots. They all run off one place. The vent stack on the island, thirty-six meters of iron coming up out of the rock in the middle of the river, with a gilt plate on its side that says it all.

DRAWN FROM RISER NINE.

Our air. Forty feet under Tier Four, somebody turns a wheel so that a Proctor need not touch the ground.

I go in the storm, because the storm is the only cover there is.

The ford is up to my thighs, and cold as a cut. Teal's pickets, the ones that did not come over, are watching the water. They do not watch the sky. Nobody up here ever has to. I go round them on my belly through the reeds, with a dead man's boots around my neck and my own blade in my teeth, and I think about one number.

Thirty-six.

The keeper's stair goes up the side of the stack. Iron rungs, wet and slick as an eel. A slate tag wired to the rail. CHECKED, a date, two initials. K.D. Somebody up here does the rounds. Somebody up here knows what a seal is.

I climb.

Wind. Rain. At ten meters I stop thinking. At twenty I stop feeling my hands. At thirty, I press my face to the iron, and the whole stack hums against my cheek. A low note. A sound I have heard my whole life. The sound of a thing carrying more air than it should.

Thirty-six. The crown.

A collar of iron, worn bright where a hundred Proctors have stood their heels. A flat place the width of a table. A wind that could take your coat.

I put the boots on it.

I stand on them.

For a long time, nothing.

Then one long breath, the way a ward fan does when the branch comes up. I feel it come up through my heels, my shins, my knees, the places the wire went in.

They light.

Blue. Pale and clean at the edge, and very bright at the root. The rain goes round it. A ring of cold blue light on the crown of the vent, and I stand in the middle of it, with a dead boy's name, and a white cloak, and my hands shaking.

They told us the boots were what made a Proctor.

They are a battery. And a stack. And a man who is not afraid of the height.

I have never been afraid of a height in my life. I came up a lift shaft.

I jump.

And I fall.

Of course I fall. One second. Two. The length of a held breath. Straight down the side of the stack, with the cloak streaming over my head and the river coming up black and fast and a sound in my throat that is not a word.

One. Two. Three.

I did not think this through.

Four. Five. Six.

Don't you dare stop breathing.

Seven. Eight.

And the boots take it.

I feel them catch, like a seal catching on a flange. The fall turns over in my stomach. The river is a hand's width under me, white and flat and fast, and I am not going down.

I am going up.

I will not tell you what it is like. I have tried. In the dark I have tried to find the word, and the only one I have is warm. The air under me is warm. It is our air. It smells like the cold crate and the ward and the inside of a leaf. The valley is spread out like a shift rota, every light a number, and I am above all of them, with a Proctor's boots on my feet, a hole where the ceiling should be, and rain on my face that tastes of nothing at all.

I laugh.

I do not remember the last time. It was a pipe, I think, and a girl who said a sun wants all its rays or it is just a spider.

I laugh, and I climb, and I come down on the ridge like a man stepping off a stair, and fall over, and get up, and fall over again.

It takes me the rest of that day to stop falling out of the sky.

I come down the ridge after dark.

The storm has gone. The camp is a ring of banked fires and sleeping bodies, and nobody is sleeping. I wear a Proctor's boots, and a Proctor's cloak with a Proctor's blood on the hem, and a gold seal on a cord around my neck, and nobody on the dais has come down to take it off me.

That is the thing. I have been waiting all night. Nobody has.

I walk into the camp.

The cache is at the far end. I have walked past it every day for thirty days. A white ribbon across the lid. A card on top. NOT TO BE OPENED BY CADETS. A RESULT MUST BE EARNED. Forty paces from seventy hungry children. They would sooner starve than be marked down.

I put the seal against the lock.

It opens.

Bread. Salve. Lamp oil. I give it out by hand, Violet and Teal, in the order they are standing. Nobody says thank you. They were never taught it. Nobody says noted, because it is the wrong word. They take it. They eat. A girl in a borrowed cloak weeps into a heel of bread and does not seem to know.

I give the salve to Oswin, last.

"It was eleven days late. For the leg. The Proctors said, a result must be earned."

"I know."

"Do you think it will set?"

"I think you have walked on it for five days and nobody has fallen over."

He takes it.

I turn round.

They are all behind me.

I did not call them. I gave no order. Forty of Violet and thirty of Teal have come up out of the mud and the dark and stood in a line in the firelight, in the order they happened to be standing, and they are looking at me. At the cloak. At the white.

Not at the House. Not at the name on my papers. At the cloak.

I have thought since that this is all a Proctor ever was. A man, and something white to stand in.

I have thought since that it is a great deal more dangerous than a halberd.

"Sorrel. What do we do now?"

I look at the valley. The dais. The island with its blue ring of light going out like a tier at last bell. Forty paces away a cache stands open and empty, and in the morning the Proctors will come down in white and find a man in their cloak, and nobody has taught them what to do about that.

They did not follow me because I won.

They followed me because I came back.

"One will think of something."

I hear it. The one. Three years of Ilse, and I will always say it under my breath.

But under it, in the back of my throat, where the wire never went, a different word comes up. And for the first time in thirty-one days, it is we.

END OF CHAPTER.
